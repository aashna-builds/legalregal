# Pharma / Medical Device / Aesthetics Contract Risk Playbook

Reference detail for the contract-review skill. Consult the section for the detected agreement type, then apply the general benchmarks. This is general industry practice, not Hugel-specific policy and not legal advice — it exists to make risk-flagging specific and grounded instead of generic.

## How to use this file

For each risk category below, look for the specific clause patterns and red flags listed, and compare any numbers in the contract (caps, percentages, notice periods) against the "typical range" given. Flag anything outside the typical range, anything undefined that should be defined, and anything asymmetric (one-sided in the counterparty's favor) as a genuine concern — don't flag things that are simply industry-standard.

---

## Territory & exclusivity

**Look for:** a defined territory (by country list, region, or "worldwide"), whether exclusivity is granted, carve-outs (existing customers, named accounts, e-commerce, government/institutional channels), and whether exclusivity is contingent on hitting minimums.

**Typical range:** exclusive distributor territories are usually tied to a performance floor — losing exclusivity (not the whole contract) on a first miss, termination only after 2 consecutive periods of material shortfall (commonly 70-80% of target).

**Red flags:**
- "Worldwide" or an undefined territory with no named countries/regions
- Exclusivity with no minimum-performance tie-back at all (company gets locked out of a territory indefinitely regardless of counterparty's actual sales)
- No carve-out for direct sales to existing house accounts or government/institutional tenders
- Automatic territory expansion language with no corresponding new minimums

## Minimum purchase commitments

**Look for:** whether minimums are annual or per-period, whether they escalate year over year, what happens on a shortfall (true-up payment, loss of exclusivity, termination right), and whether force majeure or regulatory delay excuses a miss.

**Typical range:** year-1 minimums are often lower/ramp-up, with meaningful escalation (commonly 15-30% YoY) starting year 2; shortfall remedies are usually tiered (cure period → loss of exclusivity → termination), not immediate termination.

**Red flags:**
- Flat or aggressively escalating minimums with no ramp-up period, especially for a new/pre-commercial market entry
- Immediate termination right on a single missed period with no cure period
- No carve-out for delays caused by the company's own regulatory approval timeline (e.g., FDA clearance/510(k)/PMA delays) when the distributor's minimum depends on product availability
- Minimums stated in units with no currency/price adjustment mechanism, or vice versa

## Product liability & indemnification

**Look for:** which party indemnifies for product liability, defect, and IP infringement claims; whether indemnification is capped and at what amount/multiple (e.g., fees paid, insurance limits); insurance minimums required of each party; and whether indemnification is mutual or one-sided.

**Typical range:** manufacturer typically indemnifies for product defects/design; distributor/HCP typically indemnifies for misuse, off-label promotion, or unauthorized modification; product liability insurance minimums in aesthetics/medical device commonly run $2-5M per occurrence / $5-10M aggregate for mid-size distributors.

**Red flags:**
- One-sided indemnification (only the smaller/weaker party indemnifies, or the manufacturer indemnifies for the counterparty's own promotional conduct)
- No cap on indemnification exposure at all (uncapped liability is a material risk to flag regardless of which side it favors)
- Indemnification cap that's suspiciously low relative to deal size (e.g., capped at one month of purchases)
- No insurance requirement, or insurance requirement with no proof-of-coverage/certificate obligation
- Indemnification triggered by "any claim" rather than a defined, reasonably scoped set of claims

## IP / trademark licensing

**Look for:** what's licensed (trademark only, vs. trademark + trade dress + marketing materials), scope (specific SKUs/territory vs. broad), quality-control rights (approval over how the mark is used), ownership of improvements/adaptations/local marketing materials created by the licensee, and post-termination phase-out rights for existing inventory/materials bearing the mark.

**Typical range:** licensor retains ownership of the mark and all derivative/adapted materials; licensee gets a non-exclusive or exclusive-to-territory right to use approved materials; post-termination sell-off/phase-out period for branded inventory is commonly 90-180 days.

**Red flags:**
- Licensee retains ownership of adaptations, translations, or local marketing collateral built on the licensed mark
- No quality-control/approval mechanism over how the mark is used (risk to brand consistency and to trademark validity itself)
- No post-termination phase-out period, forcing an abrupt halt or, worse, no obligation to stop using the mark at all
- Trademark license silently bundled into a broader "all IP" grant without itemizing what's actually included

## Regulatory compliance representations

**Look for:** representations regarding regulatory status (FDA clearance/approval, CE mark, or local-market equivalent) as a condition of the deal; restrictions on off-label promotion; adverse event / complaint reporting obligations and timelines between the parties; recall cooperation; and representations that marketing materials will be reviewed/approved before use.

**Typical range:** adverse event information is typically required to be passed to the manufacturer within 24-48 hours of the counterparty becoming aware, to meet the manufacturer's own regulatory reporting clocks; off-label promotion is typically prohibited outright, not just "discouraged."

**Red flags:**
- No adverse event / complaint reporting obligation on the counterparty at all, or a reporting window too long to meet the manufacturer's own regulatory deadlines
- No off-label promotion restriction, or restriction that's advisory rather than a binding obligation with consequences
- No requirement that marketing/promotional materials be pre-approved by the manufacturer
- No recall cooperation clause (counterparty obligated to stop distribution/sale and assist with recall logistics)
- Regulatory approval representations that are one-sided (only the manufacturer represents regulatory good-standing; the distributor makes none about its own licenses to distribute/sell in-market)

## Confidentiality scope

**Look for:** definition of confidential information (marked vs. unmarked, or "all information disclosed" default), standard carve-outs (independently developed, publicly available, rightfully known before disclosure, required by law/subpoena), duration of the obligation, and return/destruction obligations at termination.

**Typical range:** confidentiality survival period commonly 3-5 years post-termination (trade secrets often indefinite); standard four carve-outs present; return-or-destroy obligation within 30 days of termination or written request.

**Red flags:**
- No carve-outs at all (everything disclosed is "confidential" forever, including public information)
- Definition so broad it would restrict the counterparty from using its own general industry knowledge going forward
- No expiration on the confidentiality obligation where trade secret status isn't actually claimed (indefinite confidentiality for ordinary business information is unusual and often unenforceable, but still worth flagging as unusual)
- No mutual obligation (only one party's information is protected) in an agreement where both sides plainly exchange sensitive information

## Termination

**Look for:** termination for convenience (with what notice period) vs. termination for cause (what constitutes cause, cure period), post-termination obligations (inventory buy-back or sell-off, non-solicitation, data/material return, transition assistance), and whether termination rights are symmetric between the parties.

**Typical range:** termination for convenience notice commonly 90-180 days in distributor/vendor agreements; cure periods for a curable breach commonly 30 days; asymmetric termination-for-cause triggers (e.g., manufacturer can terminate for the distributor's regulatory violation, but not vice versa) are common and often appropriate, but should still be surfaced.

**Red flags:**
- No cure period at all for curable breaches
- Termination for convenience available to only one party
- No wind-down/transition provisions (abrupt cutoff with active inventory, patients, or open orders in the field)
- Automatic renewal ("evergreen") with a very short window to opt out (e.g., a 30-day notice requirement buried far ahead of a multi-year renewal date)

## Governing law

**Look for:** choice of law, venue/forum, and dispute resolution mechanism (litigation vs. arbitration, and if arbitration, administering body and seat).

**Typical range:** for a US-headquartered company contracting with international distributors, governing law is often the company's home jurisdiction with arbitration (rather than litigation) as the resolution mechanism, given cross-border enforcement is easier for arbitral awards (under the New York Convention) than foreign court judgments.

**Red flags:**
- Governing law/venue set in the counterparty's home jurisdiction with no arbitration alternative, especially for a smaller or newer market entity
- No dispute resolution mechanism specified at all (defaults to whatever litigation is available in the named venue, which may be slow or unfavorable)
- Waiver of jury trial or class action absent without the executive being flagged that this is what's happening (informational, not necessarily a problem)

## Notes by agreement type

- **Distributor agreements:** territory/exclusivity and minimum purchase commitments are usually the highest-stakes categories; regulatory compliance reps matter because the distributor is often the one directly facing HCPs/regulators in-market.
- **HCP / practice agreements:** off-label promotion and adverse-event reporting are the highest-stakes categories; territory/exclusivity and minimum purchase commitments often don't apply the same way (flag as not applicable rather than forcing an answer) — but look for volume-based rebate/discount structures that function like minimum commitments.
- **Vendor / supply agreements:** product liability/indemnification and IP ownership of any custom work product are usually highest-stakes; regulatory compliance reps matter if the vendor handles/manufactures anything subject to FDA oversight (e.g., a contract manufacturer or packager).
- **Licensing agreements:** IP/trademark scope and quality control are the core of the deal; look closely at royalty/milestone payment structures functioning like minimum commitments, and at sublicensing rights.
- **NDAs:** confidentiality scope and duration are the whole document; territory, minimums, and indemnification typically won't apply — don't force those sections, just note they're not applicable to this agreement type.
